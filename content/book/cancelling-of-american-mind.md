The Canceling of the American Mind: Cancel Culture Undermines Trust and Threatens Us All—But There Is a Solution
by Greg Lukianoff

⭐️⭐️⭐️⭐️⭐️

I am going to provide some of my political background and personal experiences because I think it is helpful context for anyone who considers picking this book up. Feel free to skip down to my short review at the bottom! (I got slightly lazy towards the end)

<b>Political Background</b>
I'm a cis-white male athiest (agnostic depending on how you define) cultural and political leftist (market socialist even?) and I agree with the left on a majority of issues. I was raised in Florida in a lower-class gun-toting right-libertarian culture at home and a school of culturally left students (generally). I'm 21 at the time of writing this and I'm currently attending an elite college.

<b>Personal Experiences</b>
(Note: I'm going to talk mostly about experiences from the left, but the right isn't much better, especially with their disinformation and dismissal of experts and data and literally saying that I've been brainwashed and indoctrinated.)

I have felt a mounting essentialist culture in the United States where people are quick to judge and ostracize people for their beliefs and will not engage in an open good-faith conversation about sensitive issues, leading to a superficial and fragile consensus on important topics. It's a cynical view that's passed off as wisdom. It's tribalism passed off as moral superiority. It's hard to have a level-headed conversation about sex, gender, and trans people with friends. It's hard to talk about issues men have or transhumanist ideas without being categorized into a group I share nothing in common with. It's hard to express beliefs about race and identity without others discrediting me on behalf of my own race and identity. I see friends turn on friends for a problematic statement or joke without follow up and complete lack of trust in the other person's moral or intellectual character and ability to change. I think I've become better about being authentic and up-spoken with time, but I can't say the political climate isn't a barrier and it has made the conversational landscape a minefield.

90% of the times "woke" is used, it is used profoundly stupid way equivalent to "libtard" meant to shut down people with leftist views (especially by politicians). 90% of the times "cancelling" is used, it is used to minimize accountability for harmful actions (partially by alluding to some kind of conspiracy?) (ex: Trump). But I do think there is an aspect of "cancel culture" that is real and harmful and unjustifiably triggered by certain actions.

Perhaps part of this is just "the world used to be better than it is" bias that comes with time or perhaps in youth, friends and adults are less judgemental and more likely to have open and authentic conversations. Or maybe this is just me being socially anxious.

<b>Review:</b>
While I believe this book was a bit too essentialist in its own right (using heavier morally-coated language than needed, alluding to old dogshit platitudes, using more extreme anecdotes than the average-case, and using anecdotes referring to an event where I feel like this person should have been deplatformed) I think it does illuminate higher education, the history of censorship, and some of the mechanisms by which people are cancelled (Also I read that there are some issues with cherry picking data and other things? I haven't evaluated this for myself.) I particularly liked: The anecdotes, discussion of the class-shift of journalism, offense archeology, underdog preference, COVID and the distrust of experts, denouncement of Trump's sewing of distrust, the coddling of children and asking them to have authorities solve their issues, the bloat of administration/investigations and political litmus tests in higher education and research, and conversations on free speech law vs culture and why culture is important. There was a lot of interesting commentary on class and the top-down enforcement of leftist political ideology from academic administrators and donors. Overall, I would highly recommend this short read despite its issues.

(I would also highly recommend ContraPoints' video on Cancelling to supplement this book.)
